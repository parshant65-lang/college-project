📝 CLEAN ARTICLE BODY:

Come November, Voyager 1 will be around one light-day away from the earth. That means light will take one day to travel from our home planet to the spacecraft.

The Voyager missions are two identical spacecraft NASA launched in 1977 to explore the outer planets. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 made historic flybys of Jupiter and Saturn; Voyager 2 also visited Uranus and Neptune. Both spacecraft later became humankind’s most distant operating probes. Today, Voyager 1 is at the outer limits of the solar system, and has been for a few years.

The spacecraft had to travel at 17 km/s on average for roughly 17,900 days just to get to the picket fence at the end of its little neighbourhood — a day’s journey for light. On the other hand, our telescopes have captured breathtaking visuals of distant celestial wonders, from the Pillars of Creation to the Orion Nebula, from the Sombrero Galaxy to Hoag’s Object, thousands to millions of lightyears away. It is entirely possible humans may never see them up close firsthand, but who knows what the future will bring?

In his 2010 book Voyager: Exploration, Space, and the Third Great Age of Discovery, historian Stephen Pyne divides writers on exploration into “lumpers” (who look for grand, unifying patterns across history) and “splitters” (who focus on the unique, specific characteristics of individual expeditions, nations, or eras). The proto-lumpers, he says, hold that humans’ urge to explore lies somewhere in the genome itself; that it is providential. But where Pyne’s book really shines is when it takes a closer look at those who elected to not explore, unearthing how history shaped the ways in which we think about exploration today.

Ming China launched seven magnificent fleets before curtailing oceangoing. He writes, “Medieval Islam sponsored great travellers before shrinking into the ritual pilgrimage of the haj. The Norse spanned the Atlantic, then withered on the fjords of Greenland.” According to Pyne, exploration is not some kind of anthropological necessity but an institution sustained by certain rivalries. Like the Aborigines of Oceania, he went on, “Plenty of peoples … felt little urgency to search beyond the daunting seas or looming peaks.”

It can be a dispiriting notion. Exploration is what we do when we rise above the squalor and the misery. Or is it? Pyne guides readers to a more special, and somehow more comforting, thought: that exploration could be all that it has been purported to be if it provides a journey into a wider moral universe that helps us understand who a people are and how they should behave. And you can have this experience as much on the shores of Neptune’s rings as with, to borrow from Rainer Maria Rilke, the “great wide sounds” of the sea crashing down over you at the beach.

The Interstellar Age, by planetary scientist Jim Bell, recounts a kindred example: the story of how Voyager 1 nearly did not take the Pale Blue Dot image. The picture, for which Voyager 1 had to turn back towards the sun while risking damage to its cameras so it could photograph the earth, had no scientific content — yet it is arguably the mission’s most consequential yield. The astronomer Carl Sagan, upon beholding it, came to his famous conclusion about how we should treat each other.

Scarcity is an important part of how we have come to understand exploration: we are told the mind can afford to rise above squalor only after there is no squalor at all, that we must choose between bread or roses.

But Bell explains how Voyager 1 itself cost only around one dime per American person per year over its lifetime. Almost always, there is enough for bread as well as roses. This goes for India, too, where necessitous spending by the government on the space programme has become something of a bogeyman.

In fact, as the Pale Blue Dot image, among many others, illustrated, the mission delivered to humans an inestimable gain: a sense of place in the universe. As if extending that sense, Murmurs of Earth: The Voyager Interstellar Record is pointed in its assertion that the Voyagers’ Golden Records were designed to “join a commonwealth of space-faring societies”.

Each Golden Record was a copper disc plated with gold, containing sounds, music, images, and greetings from the earth in 55 languages (including Tamil and Telugu), plus instructions to locate the planet.

For being co-written by Sagan, who also chaired the committee that selected the Records’ contents, Murmurs remains a relatively obscure book, in part because it went out of print two decades ago. Its text recounts how the Golden Records were made and why they contain the information they do, written by the people who built them. And it says that this team agreed to exclude mentions of war, disease, crime, and poverty.

As the American science journalist Jon Lomberg (who also co-authored Murmurs) says in The Interstellar Age about what kind of influence the team wanted the Records to have: “It was like asking ourselves, ‘Could we measure up to the Voyager Record?’ This is us at our best, or at least not at our worst. This approach made the record aspirational…”.

But if Voyager is any guide, the aspiration is also paradoxical: if rivalry is the most reliable engine of a great age of exploration, a pacified, egalitarian world simply may not explore. In the end, nothing can implicitly justify the impulse to explore because it is never just an impulse. It is always purposeful, and thus needs to be collectively owned, internationally authored, unenclosed, and answerable to the people paying for it, even if — or especially if — it inspires them.
