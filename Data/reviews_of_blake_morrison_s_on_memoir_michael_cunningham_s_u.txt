📝 CLEAN ARTICLE BODY:

We live in an era of oversharing: social media bombards us with personal details that can drive us to tears. The memoir, cringy when it is not boring, might become redundant. An autobiography is only to be trusted, wrote George Orwell, when it reveals something disgraceful. The opposite may be truer today. It is as if no life without child abuse or a murder in the family is worth writing about.

“It is no great pleasure to have lived,” wrote Lord Byron, “and less to live over again the details of existence — but the last becomes a necessity and even a duty.” In On Memoir: An A-Z of Life Writing, Blake Morrison comments, “The necessity is telling the story; the duty, to tell it honestly.”

It might be churlish to criticise a memoir. A stranger is laying out their life for us, and we must tread softly lest we crush it. They are saying either, “This is who I am,” or “This is who I wanted to be, so let’s pretend this is who I am.” Both reveal truths.

Michael Cunningham’s 2024 novel Day begins thus: “If Wolfe were real, he’d be the elusive figure at the heart of the story.” Cunningham, an underrated writer despite the Pulitzer Prize for The Hours, is the elusive figure at the heart of his own story, Unsayable: A Life in Writing. He writes with a vividity that somehow manages to render the overall picture blurry, remaining just out of reach as a person although strongly present as a writer.

Cunningham places aspects of his life in a petri dish for us to better understand his fiction and non-fiction. Sometimes it is difficult to differentiate between the two in some anecdotes — from the Tupperware story with which he begins the book, through his many gay encounters. Facts are the scaffolding on which higher truths rest, what Morrison calls “psychological truth and emotional resonance”. Cunningham believes that fiction is for getting at the truth when the truth isn’t sufficient for the truth. “No story is purely, literally autobiographical, including autobiographies,” writes Cunningham, and asks, “Who’s the most reliable source about anyone’s past — the person who lived it, or the person who witnessed it? It’s probably both.” Morrison quotes Engligh playwright Harold Pinter: “The real truth is that there is never any such thing as one truth…” Memoir-writing can be the work of a lifetime. Ask Karl Ove Knausgård (six volumes), Ved Mehta (12), or Augusten Burroughs (eight). Writing on memoir-writing with clarity and joy while sharing a treasure chest of experiences is a distillation of a lifetime’s work.

The memoirist knows how the story began and where it will end; the best are aware of the distance in time and maturity between the person who experienced an event and the one writing about it years later. They know how to play on that tension without bringing a later knowledge to make a previous event seem inevitable.

Hindsight can connect the scattered dots of one’s life, but the memoirist should be careful not to pretend that the dots were always connected. Hindsight can make the past appear inevitable. Yet, a slightly different pattern imposed on the dots can be a literary version of the Butterfly effect when a small change in an initial condition can lead to massive differences in later life.

Much of the power of Unsayable is in the first three quarters of the book: it is when Cunningham reads Virginia Woolf’s Mrs Dalloway in school; when his actions in youth are detailed without embarrassment and connected to the writerly life. He takes the reader into confidence, lighting areas of his own soul, confident that what he did when young would not be held against the older person. Some of that electricity and shock leak out towards the end, however, with the connections sometimes appearing forced.

Shock is an integral part of the memoir, says Morrison, in a book that invites dipping in and out. Perhaps the most significant aspect of the memoir — as opposed to the novel — is that it is safe from AI, “which can’t speak authentically, in the first person as an embodied human being”, says Morrison. “Memoir tells the truth about a real life. And AI isn’t alive… when AI takes over literature, memoir will be the last genre standing.”

Memoir is more than what is said or unsaid; it is a record of the unsayable, of what language can and cannot do. Being fully aware of this, Morrison and Cunningham who are in their 70s, make for excellent guides on a young writer’s journey. All writing is a matter of choice, they seem to be saying — what to retain, what to leave out — and it does not matter whether it is fiction or nonfiction. The lessons are in the writing itself.

The reviewer’s latest book is ‘Why Don’t You Write Something I Might Read?’
